Purpose. This is the canonical structure for future Paper Review posts on this site. It combines the explanatory style of the InterEvolve and decMHT reviews with a stricter review framework that separates what the paper claims from what the released evidence actually supports.

2. Review principle

Claim ≠ evidence. Keep the authors' claimed contributions separate from your own assessment of whether the experiments, released code, datasets, and hardware results actually support those claims.

The review should follow three phases:

  1. Understand the paper: bibliographic context, problem, contributions, method, and training/inference pipeline.
  2. Check the evidence: experiments, ablations, hidden assumptions, reproducibility, and result validity.
  3. Decide what it means: research impact, engineering takeaways, reproduction experiments, related work, and final verdict.

4. One-paragraph summary

In one compact paragraph, answer: what problem is solved, what is the central idea, what changed versus prior work, and what is the strongest reported result?

Core idea: summarize the central technical move in one or two sentences.

5. Optional KPI snapshot

Use 3–4 memorable numbers only when they help establish scale, performance, generalization, or deployment evidence.

[Metric]What this number means
[Scale]Robots / tasks / data / model size
[Result]Main reported improvement
[Hardware]Real-world platform or deployment

6. Research problem

  • Prior limitation: what existing methods fail to handle.
  • Research question: what the paper is explicitly trying to answer.
  • Task assumptions: observations, actions, embodiment, supervision, simulator, object model, coordination assumptions, etc.

7. Claimed contributions

  1. Claimed contribution 1
  2. Claimed contribution 2
  3. Claimed contribution 3

State the authors' claims faithfully here. Do not judge them yet; evaluate them later under Result validity.

8. Method

8.1 Architecture / core abstraction

Introduce the system decomposition or representation before equations.

8.2 Inputs / outputs

ComponentInputOutputRole
[Module][Observation / state / tokens][Action / latent / command][Why it exists]

8.3 Objective functions / reward / optimization target

Include only equations that are needed to understand the method.

$$\mathcal{L}=\cdots$$

Variables: define important symbols and explain how the objective affects system behavior.

8.4 Dataset and training

Document dataset, simulator, collection procedure, initialization, algorithm, curriculum, losses/rewards, compute, parallelism, fine-tuning stages, and frozen vs. trainable components.

Implementation detail worth remembering: record the practical choice most likely to matter for reproduction.

8.5 Inference procedure

Describe the runtime flow step by step. Clarify what information is available at deployment time and which components run online.

9. Experiments and results

Use the smallest set of results needed to judge the central claim. Separate simulation-only evidence, real-world evidence, and author-defined internal benchmarks.

Benchmark / robotSplit / settingMetricBaselineProposedNotes
[Benchmark][Setting][Metric]——[Interpretation / caveat]

9.1 Generalization / ablation / scaling

Include the experiment that most directly tests the central mechanism: unseen objects, larger team sizes, new tasks, real hardware, robustness, or ablation.

10. Critical review

10.1 Strengths

  • Strong technical idea
  • Strong experimental evidence
  • Useful engineering or research property

10.2 Weaknesses

  • Methodological limitation
  • Evaluation limitation
  • Deployment or scalability limitation

10.3 Hidden assumptions

  • Privileged state / calibration / object model / fixed controller / known task structure
  • Assumption that may not hold outside the reported benchmark

12. Result validity

  • Fair comparison: are baselines trained and evaluated under comparable conditions?
  • Statistics: are sample count, variance, confidence intervals, or significance adequate?
  • Sim / real gap: which claims are supported on hardware rather than simulation only?
  • Benchmark independence: is the benchmark public/standard or internally constructed?
  • Metric validity: does the metric actually represent task success or deployment usefulness?
  • Claim-to-evidence match: which claims are strongly supported, partially supported, or still speculative?

13. Why this paper matters

Explain what capability or research direction changes, which assumptions are relaxed, and where the work fits relative to VLA, RL, humanoid control, manipulation, sim-to-real, or other relevant areas.

14. Engineering takeaways

  • Reusable idea: what to borrow for another system.
  • Implementation lesson: practical recipe worth remembering.
  • Deployment implication: what changes for a real robot/system.
  • Main caveat: what must not be overlooked.

15. Experiments to reproduce

  1. Minimum experiment needed to verify the core claim
  2. Ablation needed to isolate the central mechanism
  3. Real-world or out-of-distribution experiment worth running

16. Related papers

  • Closest predecessor and how it differs
  • Competing approach
  • Follow-up or complementary work

17. Final verdict

ImpactHigh / Medium / Low — why
ConfidenceHigh / Medium / Low — confidence in the evidence
Usefulness for current workHigh / Medium / Low — specific relevance

Bottom line: end with a short paragraph separating what the paper convincingly establishes from what remains unproven.

18. References

  1. Paper title, authors, venue/year.
  2. Official project page.
  3. arXiv abstract.
  4. arXiv HTML paper.
  5. Official code repository / conference page when available.
Writing rule. Keep reported facts, author claims, and your interpretation visibly separate. Attribute external figures and numerical results, reconstruct diagrams when useful, and explicitly state uncertainty when the source material does not support a stronger conclusion.
