Template · Paper Review

Purpose. This is the canonical structure for future Paper Review posts on this site. It combines the strongest patterns used in the InterEvolve and decMHT reviews: a concise thesis up front, visual/quantitative evidence, mathematical detail, implementation context, critical interpretation, and explicit limitations.

1. Required front matter

Every new Paper Review should start with the following metadata. The shared post layout automatically renders the standardized source header from these fields.

2. Opening: explain the paper in two or three paragraphs

The first paragraphs should answer three questions immediately: what problem is being solved, what is the central idea, and why is the result interesting? Avoid beginning with implementation details. A reader should understand the paper's thesis before encountering notation.

Core idea: summarize the paper's main technical move in one or two sentences. This box should be understandable even if the reader skips the rest of the article.

3. Optional KPI snapshot

When the paper has a few memorable experimental claims, surface them early. Use only numbers that materially help the reader understand scale, performance, generalization, or deployment.

[Metric]What this number means
[Scale]Robots / tasks / data / model size
[Result]Main reported improvement
[Hardware]Real-world platform or deployment

4. Problem and motivation

Describe why the problem is hard and what breaks in prior approaches. Make the baseline assumptions explicit. For robotics papers, this often means clarifying observation/action spaces, contact or embodiment constraints, coordination assumptions, and the gap between simulation and hardware.

5. Core abstraction / architecture

Introduce the paper's main representation or system decomposition before diving into equations. Prefer a reconstructed diagram when it communicates the architecture more clearly than prose.

6. Mathematical formulation

Include only equations that help explain the method. Define every symbol that is necessary for understanding the argument and connect each equation back to its physical or algorithmic meaning.

Example:

$$ \mathbf{y}=f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}), $$

where $\mathbf{x}$ is the relevant state/observation, $f_\theta$ is the learned or optimized model, and $\mathbf{y}$ is the control/policy/task output. The paragraph after an equation should explain why this equation matters, not merely restate it.

6.1 Key mechanism

Break complicated methods into H3 subsections. The Contents panel automatically includes H2 and H3 headings, so descriptive subsection names make long technical reviews much easier to navigate.

7. Training / optimization / system pipeline

Document the practical recipe: algorithm, curriculum, losses or rewards, data flow, simulator, model initialization, parallelism, compute, fine-tuning stages, and any frozen vs. trainable components. Highlight choices that explain the reported behavior.

Implementation detail worth remembering: capture one practical choice that would matter if you attempted to reproduce or adapt the work.

8. Experiments and results

Use a compact table for the most decision-relevant results. Do not reproduce every number from the paper. Prefer a smaller table that makes the central comparison obvious.

Method / settingMain metricSecondary metricInterpretation
Baseline——What the baseline reveals
Proposed——Why the improvement matters

8.1 Generalization / ablation / scaling

Include the experiment that best tests the paper's central claim: unseen objects, larger team sizes, new tasks, real hardware, ablations, or robustness. Report enough context to avoid overstating the result.

9. Why this paper matters

This section is analytical rather than descriptive. Explain what capability or research direction the paper changes, which assumptions it relaxes, and where it fits relative to VLA, RL, humanoid control, manipulation, sim-to-real, or other relevant areas.

10. Limitations and open questions

  • Assumptions: What privileged information, fixed controller, simulator, object model, or task structure is required?
  • Evaluation: Which settings are still missing?
  • Scalability: What grows poorly with tasks, robots, data, or compute?
  • Real-world gap: Which results remain simulation-only?
  • Research question: What would be the most useful next experiment?

11. Takeaways

  • One-sentence thesis: the main contribution in plain technical language.
  • Most important mechanism: the idea worth reusing elsewhere.
  • Strongest evidence: the experiment that best supports the claim.
  • Main caveat: the limitation that most changes how the result should be interpreted.

12. References

  1. Paper title, authors, venue/year.
  2. Official project page.
  3. arXiv abstract.
  4. arXiv HTML paper.
  5. Official code repository / conference page when available.
Writing rule. Keep reported facts separate from interpretation. Attribute external figures and numerical results, reconstruct diagrams when useful, and always end with limitations rather than presenting the paper as universally solved.
