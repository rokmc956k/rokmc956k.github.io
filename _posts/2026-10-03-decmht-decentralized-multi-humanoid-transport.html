Paper review · Multi-humanoid loco-manipulation

decMHT (Decentralized Multi-Humanoid Transport) is a reinforcement-learning framework for cooperative humanoid manipulation in which every robot runs the same local policy, receives only a local object attachment specification and a transformed object-motion command, and coordinates through the shared payload rather than explicit inter-robot communication.

The striking result is not just that two humanoids can carry a box. The same controller, trained with at most two robots, is evaluated zero-shot with teams of up to ten humanoids, unseen payload geometries, payloads as heavy as 50 kg, and robot-to-robot handovers, followed by hardware transfer to Digit V3 robots.

1. Why this problem is difficult

Multi-humanoid transport combines several problems that are individually hard: whole-body balance, locomotion, contact formation, load support, object tracking, and interaction forces generated by teammates. A centralized controller can in principle observe all robots and optimize their joint action, but its input and action spaces grow with team size and its coordination structure becomes tied to a particular robot configuration.

decMHT instead asks a stronger question: can one controller be defined around the object interface rather than the global multi-robot system, so that each humanoid solves only its own local contribution?

Core abstraction: do not tell each robot the full payload geometry or the states of the other robots. Tell it where its local attachment region is, what the payload should do locally, and whether it should participate.

2. Object-centric attachment specification

For robot r, the local attachment specification is

$$\mathcal{A}^{(r)} = \left({}^{b}T^{(r)},\, w^{(r)},\, d^{(r)}\right).$$

Here, $${}^{b}T^{(r)}\in SE(3)$$ is the attachment-frame pose relative to the robot torso frame, $w^{(r)}$ gives symmetric lateral hand-contact offsets, and $d^{(r)}$ is the frontal clearance used to position the torso during approach. The key design choice is that the representation is local: it does not attempt to encode the full object mesh.

Conceptual diagram reconstructed for this review. The actual method assigns one local attachment frame to each robot.

3. One payload command, many local commands

The task-level payload command is

$$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{pl}} = (v_x, v_y, \omega_z, h),$$

representing desired planar velocity, yaw rate, and payload height. Each robot converts this shared object command into a command at its own attachment frame. If $p^{(r)}_{\mathrm{offset}}$ is the fixed vector from the payload reference frame to robot r's local command frame, then

$$v^{(r)}_{\mathrm{cf}} = v_{\mathrm{pl}} + \omega_{\mathrm{pl}} \times p^{(r)}_{\mathrm{offset}},$$

$$\omega^{(r)}_{\mathrm{cf}} = \omega_{\mathrm{pl}}, \qquad h^{(r)}_{\mathrm{cf}} = h_{\mathrm{pl}} + p^{(r)}_{\mathrm{offset},z}.$$

This is an elegant decomposition. The planner reasons about one rigid object, while each robot receives the local motion it should induce at its attachment point.

4. Hierarchical decentralized controller

Each humanoid runs a high-level loco-manipulation policy and a frozen low-level locomotion policy at 50 Hz. The low-level policy maps proprioception and base-motion targets to leg joint targets:

$$q^{(r)}_{\mathrm{leg}} = \pi_l\left(s^{(r)}, v_x^{(r)}, v_y^{(r)}, \omega_z^{(r)}, h^{(r)}\right).$$

The high-level policy outputs arm targets together with desired base motion:

$$\left(q^{(r)}_{\mathrm{arm}},v_x^{(r)},v_y^{(r)},\omega_z^{(r)},h^{(r)}\right) = \pi_h\left(s^{(r)},\mathcal{A}^{(r)},\mathcal{C}^{(r)}_{\mathrm{cf}},m^{(r)}\right).$$

5. Mode-gated behavior: idle and track

A binary mode flag $m^{(r)}\in\{\texttt{idle},\texttt{track}\}$ allows one network to express multiple behaviors:

$$R^{(r)} = \mathbf{1}[m^{(r)}=\texttt{idle}]R_{\mathrm{idle}} + \mathbf{1}[m^{(r)}=\texttt{track}]R_{\mathrm{track}}.$$

6. Training with Independent PPO

The high-level policy is trained with Independent PPO (IPPO). Training begins with single-robot manipulation, then fine-tunes the same shared policy on two-robot cooperative episodes. Deployment simply runs independent copies of that shared policy.

Single-robot training uses 4096 parallel IsaacLab environments and is reported to require about 140 GPU-hours on one RTX 4090. Two-robot fine-tuning uses 2048 environments and about 60 additional GPU-hours. The policy is never trained with three or more robots.

8. Generalization to heavier and unseen objects

The controller is trained on randomized cuboid payloads with masses of only 0.5–1.5 kg, yet the evaluation sweeps payloads up to 50 kg. Larger teams progressively make heavier payloads feasible. The authors also test completely unseen geometries: a cabinet, fridge, log, bed, and concrete slab.

The bed result is the clearest ablation: pickup success rises from 44.1% after only single-robot training to 85.2% after two-robot fine-tuning. The local attachment abstraction transfers much of the manipulation behavior, but direct exposure to shared-object coupling is still important.

9. Cost of Transport

The paper evaluates mechanical efficiency using Cost of Transport:

$$\mathrm{CoT}=\frac{E}{mgd},$$

where $E$ is total mechanical energy used by the team, $m$ is payload mass, $g$ is gravity, and $d$ is payload travel distance. CoT rises sharply from one to two robots and then largely plateaus, suggesting an initial coordination overhead rather than a cost that grows continuously with team size. Two-robot fine-tuning reduces CoT across larger multi-robot teams even though those larger teams were never seen in training.

11. Sim-to-real on Digit V3

The learned controller is transferred to real Digit V3 humanoids without real-world fine-tuning. The hardware demonstrations cover:

  • single-robot pickup and transport,
  • two-robot cooperative pickup and transport,
  • robot-to-robot handover.

An external sensor provides each robot with the payload pose relative to its torso, which is used to reconstruct the local attachment-frame pose required by the policy. This external perception requirement is one of the main remaining deployment assumptions.

12. What I think is most important

The most transferable idea in decMHT is not a particular PPO reward. It is the interface decomposition. The object-level planner produces one desired payload motion; a fixed rigid-body transform turns it into local commands; a shared local controller then solves contact-rich loco-manipulation independently for every robot.

This architecture has several attractive properties for scalable humanoid systems:

  • Team-size invariance: the local policy input dimensionality does not grow with the number of robots.
  • Geometry abstraction: each robot sees an attachment slice rather than the entire object geometry.
  • Communication-free execution: coordination emerges through object dynamics and common task commands.
  • Skill composability: pickup, transport, join, leave, and handover are expressed through the same controller and mode interface.
  • Planner/controller separation: a higher-level system can choose attachment assignments and trajectories without redesigning the learned motor policy.

13. Limitations

  • The hardware setup relies on external sensing for attachment-frame tracking.
  • The experiments focus on gripperless bimanual pinching against approximately opposing surfaces.
  • All robots share a symmetric behavioral role; specialized heterogeneous roles are not represented.
  • Handover timing and mode switching are provided externally rather than discovered by the policy.
  • The policy is trained on cuboid objects, so the observed OOD geometry transfer is impressive but still depends on compatible local contact regions.

14. Takeaway

decMHT demonstrates that a carefully chosen object-centric local interface can turn a difficult centralized multi-humanoid manipulation problem into repeated instances of the same local control problem. Training with one robot builds most of the contact and lifting competence; limited two-robot fine-tuning teaches the coupled dynamics; and the resulting controller scales surprisingly far beyond its training distribution.

For multi-humanoid systems, the paper is a useful example of a broader design principle: scalability often comes more from choosing the right control abstraction than from increasing policy size or explicitly modeling every teammate.
